Learn your role.
Grow with your teammates.

For eight weeks, we will build familiarity with the same team and role, instead of changing everything each week. Our goal is better players and a better team, as well as good results.

01 / WHY

Eight weeks to build skills that last

Playing the same role repeatedly helps us learn where to receive, whom to pass to and which space to defend. We want teammates to understand each other’s movement and feel comfortable offering brief, useful advice. Team and position assignments do not rank players.

  • Individual: practise scanning before receiving, the first touch and movement after passing.
  • Team: improve spacing, cover and pass timing with teammates in the same line.
  • Organisation: cover missing players rather than reshuffle the entire team every week.

Eight weeks is our trial period. It is not a research-backed promise of a particular improvement within that time.

03 / MATCH DAY

Attendance changes. Our principles stay consistent.

  1. The final roster is what counts

    RSVPs help import names and establish duty order. Late arrivals and guest players added to the final team by the coach can also play.

  2. Keep the home team → add extra players → borrow only when needed

    Fixed members start with their original team. Extra participants with known positions are considered for suitable gaps first; guests without position information fill the remaining gaps. If one team is still short, borrow a player whose role the other team can spare for that day. Their fixed team membership stays the same.

  3. First choice → second choice → an unfilled role

    If a first choice fits, keep it. When preferences overlap, share opportunities, then consider second choices before filling other gaps. Choosing the same position twice means you want to focus on that role. We look across all periods to give as many players as possible a first-choice opportunity; this takes priority over strict three-period blocks. Attendance, manually locked positions and duty rotations can limit what is possible.

  4. Six periods: 3 + 3, or the same role throughout when possible

    We aim to keep field roles consistent within periods 1–3 and 4–6. If everyone’s roles fit, keep them in the next block too. When several players want one position, shorter blocks may be needed so someone is not left with zero first-choice periods. Goalkeeper, referee and rest rotations, or formation changes, can prevent an identical lineup in every period. Four-period matches use 2 + 2.

  5. Guests have the same playing opportunities

    Being outside the fixed plan, or having less experience, is not a reason to play less. Referee and rest duties go first to players with fewer accumulated rest periods; ties are broken by later RSVP order. One resting player across the two teams referees, alternating teams where possible. Goalkeeper duties rotate within each team and count as playing time. Dedicated goalkeepers are exempt from goalkeeper rotation.

    When numbers make equal appearances impossible, minimise the difference. For example, 23 players in an 11-a-side match over six periods play five or six periods each. The coach adjusts separately for manual changes, injuries or early departures.

  6. Short numbers and special-event matches

    With fewer than 22 players, add guest players to the teams that need them. Until they arrive, do not assume 11-a-side is possible: choose a suitable match size and check whether a separate referee is needed. For 10-a-side, 4-1-3-1 keeps fullback and attacking-midfielder positions. The older 3-4-2 has no LB, RB or AM slot; change formation to train those roles. The regular free team-allocation mode remains available for events.

04 / REVIEW

After eight weeks, discuss the next role with the coach

After the match on 25 November, discuss whether to keep or change positions and how to form the next teams. Preferences matter, but neither preference alone nor a popularity vote determines the assessment.

  • Your experience: what felt comfortable, and what was difficult in this role?
  • Progress: have positioning, scanning, passing choices and recovery runs improved?
  • Teamwork: are spacing, cover and communication better?
  • Next goal: balance your wishes with the roles the team needs.

05 / PLAYER PLAYBOOK

Understand your role, then your teammates’ roles

A formation is an agreement about how we help each other, not just dots on a pitch. Read your role first, then those of the teammates beside you. These are beginner-friendly suggestions for Barea’s 4-2-3-1. You can start by practising just one of the key habits in a match.

Four habits for everyone
  1. Look around for opponents and teammates before receiving.
  2. If you cannot turn safely, return the ball and create a new passing option.
  3. After passing, move diagonally to offer another option.
  4. When we lose the ball, the nearest player slows the opponent down; everyone else recovers towards our goal and behind the ball.
AM · Connect play behind the forward

Where? Make small movements into the space behind the opposition midfield and in front of their defence. Step out from behind an opponent so your teammate can see you. Avoid always standing on the same vertical line as the forward.

When receiving Scan both sides and decide whether to turn or return the pass. If the way ahead is open, connect with a forward or winger. If you are blocked from behind, play back to a defensive midfielder. Every pass does not need to be the difficult final ball.

When possession is lost Block the pass into their defensive midfielder before chasing all the way to their centre-backs. Stay connected to our defensive midfield.

Today’s habit “Find a visible gap, not the forward’s space.”
Useful calls “Man on”, “Turn”, “Back to me”.

DM · Offer different angles; protect the middle

Where? In front of the centre-backs, use slightly different heights and angles from the other defensive midfielder. Do not both go forward or crowd the ball. If one moves to receive, the other protects the centre.

When receiving Open your body and check the fullback, AM and other DM. A backward pass is a good option when the route forward is blocked. Do not force a turn with your back to pressure near our goal.

When possession is lost Protect central passing lanes rather than only chasing the ball. Cover behind a teammate who steps out to press.

Today’s habit “One steps out; one holds the middle.”
Useful calls “I’m covering”, “Other side”, “Time”.

CB · One challenges, one covers

Where? Stay between their forward and our goal. Move with awareness of the other centre-back and the fullbacks. If an opponent has time to look up, be ready for a pass behind us.

When receiving Look first for a reliable pass to a fullback or DM. In a dangerous area with no safe route, choose a safer clearance rather than forcing a pass across the centre.

When defending Slow down and guide the attacker wide instead of immediately sticking a foot in. If the other centre-back steps out, cover the space behind and check other attackers rather than charging at the same player.

Today’s habit “Do not both step out together.”
Useful calls “I’ll go”, “Cover me”, “Hold the line”.

LB / RB · Protect inside, connect outside

Where? When defending, stay connected to the centre-back and protect the route towards the middle of our goal. If play comes from the other side, check the winger behind you too.

When receiving Check three options: your winger, DM and centre-back. Do not overlap every time the winger receives: offering a pass back is useful too. When you go forward, tell someone to cover behind.

When possession is lost Guide the opponent outside and recover towards goal. Do not chase the ball and leave the centre open.

Today’s habit “Make a triangle; do not stand in the winger’s space.”
Useful calls “Behind you”, “Going forward”, “Cover inside”.

LW / RW · Start wide, move at the right moment

Where? Start wide to create room for teammates in the centre. When a pass is available, choose whether to receive to feet or run behind. Do not keep moving towards the ball every time.

When receiving If isolated, combine with the fullback or AM rather than always trying to dribble through. When the opposite winger crosses, enter the far-side scoring area instead of watching the ball.

When possession is lost Follow their advancing fullback so our fullback does not have to defend two opponents alone.

Today’s habit “Start wide; move when the pass is available.”
Useful calls “To feet”, “In front”, “Back to you”.

FW · Hold, connect, then move towards goal

Where? Occupy their defenders to leave space for our AM. Drop to receive when needed, but if the AM drops, do not follow into the same spot.

When receiving With your back to goal, lay the ball off to a nearby teammate and move towards goal again. Every ball does not need a turn and shot. When a teammate looks up, move between defenders or behind them.

When possession is lost Close central passing routes and guide play to one side, rather than chasing alone. Pressing disconnected from teammates behind you is easy to escape.

Today’s habit “After dropping to link play, go forward again.”
Useful calls “One-two”, “Lay-off”, “Far side”.

GK · Clear calls and safe distribution

Where? Use the line between the ball and the centre of the goal to judge your angle, and adjust as the ball moves. On crosses, judge whether you can claim the ball without forcing a collision.

With the ball Look for a safe teammate instead of rushing a throw into the centre. Check nearby opponents, where you may use your hands and the restart rules below.

Today’s habit “Call loudly when claiming; do not force a dangerous challenge.”
Useful calls “Keeper”, “Right shoulder”, “Time”. Rotating goalkeeper periods count as playing appearances.

Position videos will be added after checking that they are accessible and useful for players. For now, use the guidance above.

15 minutes together · After warming up
  1. 0–5 minutes / Triangle passing in groups of 3–4

    Place cones roughly 6–8m apart. Scan behind you, open your body and pass after your first touch. After passing, move to a free corner. Shorten the distance if control is difficult; accuracy comes before speed.

  2. 5–10 minutes / 4v2 keep-ball

    In a roughly 10×10m square, four players create passing options while two close the lanes. Beginners have no touch limit; defenders do not tackle. Rotate defenders every 45–60 seconds. Enlarge the area if possession is lost too quickly; reduce it slightly if the group succeeds.

  3. 10–15 minutes / Connect players in the same line

    Link centre-backs, DM, fullbacks and AM. Change your receiving angle after passing and call “Behind you” or “Turn”. On a signal, imagine possession has been lost: one nearby player approaches, while others cover the centre and space behind. Use the final minute to choose one habit for the match.

Distances and difficulty are starting suggestions for our team. Stop if you have pain, and do not use this practice as a substitute for warming up.

06 / PLAY STRONG, PLAY FAIR

Protect the ball with confidence.
Keep contact controlled.

Barea actively practises fair shoulder challenges and shielding. Contact itself is not automatically a foul. The aim is to keep balance and position while the ball is within playing distance, not to knock an opponent over.

Barea’s additional safety agreement: no tackles

Even tackles allowed by the official Laws are excluded in this trial. In particular, do not slide, reach in from behind or put your leg between an opponent’s legs. Distinguish intercepting a pass from forcing a challenge at an opponent’s feet. Both teams and the rotating referee confirm this before playing.

01 · Shoulder challenges and shielding

You may put your body between an opponent and the ball when the ball is within playing distance, without holding the opponent with your arms. The opponent may also challenge fairly. Shoulder contact is not automatically legal. Judge direction, speed, force and distance from the ball together.

  • Controlled contact while competing side by side for a nearby ball may be fair.
  • Running into someone from a distance, pushing their back, pushing with an elbow or arm, holding or tripping are not fair challenges.
  • Touching the ball first does not excuse a dangerous follow-through. Whether someone falls is not the only test.

Barea practice suggestion: start at walking speed with the ball beside you. Success means keeping your balance and control, not knocking the other player down.

IFAB Law 12 · Contact and shielding ↗
02 · Unintentional contact can still be a foul

Careless charging, pushing or tripping is a foul. Reckless action that disregards danger warrants a caution; excessive force or endangering an opponent warrants a sending-off. “I didn’t mean it” is not an exemption.

A high foot near someone’s head can be dangerous play even without contact. With contact, assess the direct-free-kick offence. Blocking a route away from the ball is different from simply occupying your position.

IFAB Law 12 · Dangerous play and sanctions ↗
03 · Handball: a touch on the arm is not always an offence

Consider whether the hand or arm moved towards the ball, or whether its position made the body unnaturally bigger without justification for that movement. Being close to the ball or a deflection from another body part does not automatically exempt the contact.

A player cannot score directly with their hand or arm, or immediately after the ball touches their own hand or arm, even accidentally. A goalkeeper’s permitted handling inside their own penalty area is treated separately.

Official Laws · Judging handball ↗
04 · Offside: judge when a teammate plays the ball

At the moment a teammate plays the ball, check whether the player in the opponents’ half is nearer their goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent. Level is not an offside position. Position alone is not an offence: involvement is needed, such as playing the ball or interfering with an opponent’s play or vision.

The goalkeeper is not always the last defender. A rebound from a defender does not automatically cancel offside; whether it was deliberate play requires judgement. There is no offside offence when receiving directly from a throw-in, goal kick or corner.

Without assistants, agree beforehand who judges offside and how. Players must not stop play themselves just by raising a hand.

IFAB Law 11 · Position and involvement ↗
05 · Throw-ins: both hands, behind the head, both feet grounded

Face the pitch and throw with both hands from behind and over the head, at the point where the ball went out. At release, part of each foot must touch the line or the ground outside it. Both feet off the ground or a one-handed throw is incorrect.

Opponents stay at least 2m from the throwing point. An incorrect throw gives the opponents a throw-in. The thrower cannot touch the ball again before another player touches it. You cannot score directly from a throw-in.

IFAB Law 15 · Throw-in checklist ↗
06 · Goalkeepers: back-passes and handling

Handling a ball deliberately kicked to you by a teammate, or received directly from a teammate’s throw-in, normally gives an indirect free kick. Tricks to circumvent the restriction are also prohibited. Outside your penalty area, the usual handball rules apply.

Do not challenge for a ball the goalkeeper controls with their hands or interfere as they release it to kick. Under the official Laws, control with the hands or arms for more than eight seconds gives the opponents a corner kick; the referee visibly counts the final five seconds.

Official Laws · Goalkeeper handling ↗
07 · Out of play: the whole ball must cross the line

If any part of the ball remains over the line, it is still in play. The whole ball must cross even in the air. A player standing outside does not make the ball out. A ball that hits the goal frame and stays on the pitch remains in play.

Contact with the referee does not always mean play continues. If it starts a promising attack, changes possession or results in a direct goal, restart with a dropped ball.

IFAB Law 9 · In and out of play ↗
08 · Free kicks, penalties and goal kicks

A direct free kick can go straight into the opponents’ goal; an indirect free kick must touch another player first. A raised arm signals an indirect free kick. A direct-free-kick offence inside your own penalty area gives a penalty; not every offence there is a penalty.

Opponents normally stay 9.15m away. If a defensive wall has three or more players, attackers stay at least 1m away from it. Wait if the referee has instructed a whistle restart while arranging the wall. Do not deliberately obstruct a quick restart.

A goal kick is in play once it is kicked and clearly moves. It does not need to leave the penalty area. Opponents remain outside the penalty area until the ball is in play.

IFAB Law 13 · Free kicks ↗ · IFAB Law 16 · Goal kicks ↗
09 · Rotating referees and players: one shared standard

Before starting: check numbers, goalkeepers, playing time and the no-tackle agreement. Move to see the ball and both players; do not miss holding while only watching the ball.

When deciding: judge where contact occurred, its direction and force, and distance from the ball. After the whistle, point clearly. A fall or loud appeal alone must not decide the call. Stop for a suspected serious injury. Advantage can be allowed, but do not force it in a dangerous situation.

Barea communication agreement: one representative asks briefly during a stoppage. No group protests or insults. Review unclear incidents on video after the match to align the next match’s standard. Do not pretend certainty about an incident you did not see.
